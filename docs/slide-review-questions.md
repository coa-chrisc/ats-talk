# Slide review questions

Ask these of every slide before it ships. Read the slide cold, as a Deputy CIO who has never heard of the course, the sandbox or anyone in the room. Read the title and the two story lines aloud. Then read every label on the drawing as if it were the only thing on the screen.

If any answer is "no" or "I'm not sure," rewrite the words. Never fix it by shortening, and never fix it to satisfy a test. If a test or a layout fights a well-written sentence, change the test or the layout.

## 1. Does the reader have what they need?

**Has this thing been introduced before we rely on it?**
A course, a program, a team, a tool, a person. The first draft opened with "four ideas we brought back" before saying what the course was. *Check: could someone who skipped the previous slides say what each proper noun is?*

**Is every term defined before it is used, or replaced with plain words?**
"The model," "a prompt," "allow lists," "infrastructure as code," "landing zone," "a lifecycle," "bounded autonomy," "bright lines" and "sanction" all appeared undefined. *Check: circle every word a DCIO might not use in their own meetings. Define it in the same sentence or swap it for plain English.*

**Is every acronym and name explained the first time?**
BNY needed "a bank." DCIO, CISO, BCG, Gartner, Quisitive and the Digital Cities Survey each need a few words the first time they appear, unless this audience uses them daily.

**Do pronouns point at one thing?**
"It trained its people before giving them any autonomy." Does "them" mean the people or the agents? *Check: for every it, they, them, this and that, can you name what it refers to without rereading?*

## 2. Is every thought finished?

**Does each sentence say what happens as a result?**
"Each rung up is more to govern." More of what, for whom? "Most of the money goes to one kind of work." Which kind? "In every case, a limit was missing." What limit, and on what? *Check: after each claim, ask "so what?" and "meaning what?" If the answer isn't on the slide, the thought is cut off.*

**Is the cause stated, not hinted?**
The incidents slide said what went wrong but not why: no permission limit, no spending cap, no review before customers saw it. It also needed to say plainly that none of them were hacks. *Check: for every failure or success, does the slide say what caused it?*

**Are vague adjectives made concrete?**
"Start where mistakes are small." Small how: in cost, in impact, in how easy they are to undo? *Check: for every small, big, light, heavy, strong, early or fast, ask "measured how?"*

**Are two sentences side by side actually connected?**
The BNY line put agents in one sentence and training in the next with nothing joining them. *Check: read each pair of sentences and say the link out loud ("because," "so," "and then"). If you can't, add it.*

## 3. Would a person actually say this?

**Would you say it this way to a colleague in the hallway?**
"Earn each next step with evidence," "a pathway," "nothing asked first" and "a rule the system enforces holds" are compressed phrases nobody speaks. *Check: say it aloud. If you'd rephrase it in conversation, use the rephrasing.*

**Is it a fact or a clever line?**
"A vendor who cannot answer them has already answered." "Nobody can sell it to us." "The four lessons are those limits." Each sounds like a point and says nothing checkable. *Check: if a line could go on a poster, ask what it literally claims. Write that instead.*

**Is any sentence chopped short to fit?**
Several lines were trimmed until they lost their meaning, to meet a character count. *Check: compare the slide to the spoken paragraph. If the slide is a compressed version of a sentence in the notes, use the full sentence or give it more room.*

**Do the words sound like someone narrating the slide?**
Nothing on the stage says what the slide is trying to do, reassures the audience, or describes context only the authors have. *Check: delete any phrase that describes the slide rather than the subject.*

## 4. Do the numbers make sense on their own?

**Does every number say what it is a share of, and of whom?**
"15 percent" needed "of day-to-day work decisions will be made by agents." "Six percent" needed "of companies." "17 percent" needed "of IT budgets." *Check: for each figure, can a cold reader finish the sentence "X percent of ___"?*

**Does every number say who measured it and when?**
Gartner expects, BCG found, as of October 2026. A forecast is labeled as a forecast. *Check: every figure has a source and date in the speaker notes and in the facts-to-confirm list.*

**Are chart parts labeled with what they are?**
The spending bars needed "2025" and "2026," "AI investment," and "Of IT budgets." The buy/boost/build bar needed a heading saying it shows where the money is going. *Check: cover the title and story lines. Can you still read the chart?*

**Is the same number the same everywhere?**
A figure on slide 3 that comes back on slide 11 must match, and so must anything quoted in the notes. *Check: search the script for each number.*

**Have we claimed more than the source says?**
Don't say staff built the agents if the source only says they were trained. Keep the five percent undated if the source doesn't give a year. *Check: read the claim against the source note and remove anything we inferred.*

## 5. Does the slide carry the story?

**Does the title make the point by itself?**
A title is a sentence that states the slide's claim, not a topic heading. *Check: if this were the only line someone read, would they know what we're arguing?*

**Is the point for the City stated?**
Enterprise facts only matter when we say what they mean here. Slide 3 needed "that is where the City's value lies too." *Check: does a DCIO know why this slide is in a talk about the City?*

**Does it follow from the slide before and lead to the next?**
*Check: read the last story line of the previous slide, then this title. Is it one story?*

**Is each idea called by one name all the way through?**
"Ideas" became "lessons" everywhere, or not at all. The four levels keep the same names on slides 24, 26 and 27. *Check: if a thing is renamed, search and change every place it appears.*

**Is the ask clear: who decides what?**
On the decision slides, can a DCIO tell which decision is theirs and what it costs? *Check: name the decider and the cost for each ask.*

## 6. Is it fair to the people in it?

**Is anyone we describe being treated kindly and accurately?**
The authorized-tools policy is the right first move, not a mistake. The departments that did the work get the credit. *Check: would the CISO, a department director or anyone quoted be comfortable seeing this slide with their boss in the room?*

**Have the quoted people agreed, and are their titles current?**
*Check: the note on the slide lists who to tell before it's shown.*

**Does the slide avoid implying blame we haven't earned?**
"The City is behind" is said kindly and with the reason: no budget line, not bad people.

## 7. Does it fit and look right?

**Does every line fit with nothing running off the edge?**
Slide 10's title ran off the page. *Check: view the rendered still, not just the test results.*

**Does each line break at a natural place?**
A line that ends on "The" or splits a phrase in two reads badly. Rewrite the sentence before accepting a bad break.

**Is the layout different from the slides around it?**
Avoid a run of rounded cards. Each picture should show how the thing works, not just three boxes with words in them.

**Can every label stand alone?**
"Strongest" needed "control." "Evidence" became "Results." *Check: read each label by itself. Does it say something?*
