## What the DCIOs should walk away with

1. Four lessons from the course they can repeat in a meeting, and the sense that they understood each one.
2. An accurate picture of where the City stands: where it looks like it is on paper, where it is once its own work is counted and the outside recognition it already has, and where it could be with organization and a budget line. The program walks into a community; it does not start one.
3. A plan whose first steps are already half done, so each decision reads as a short distance rather than a request.
4. A clear sense of their own part: leadership sets the bright lines, the DCIO decides authority, the CISO owns the controls.
5. Confidence in the people who have been doing this work, and in Stefan and Chris as the ones to organize it. The talk never asks for that; the last slide shows that the plan they just heard was the course applied. The one thing only leadership can add is the connection between the pieces, and the talk hands them that as the decision and the credit.

## The story

Two people went to a course and came back with four lessons. Before teaching the lessons, they show what the lessons answer: enterprise has moved to agents, spent heavily, changed how it reviews work, and stumbled wherever a limit was missing. Then the four lessons, one at a time. Then the City: on paper it is at the start line, but once you count the people, the recognition and the infrastructure, it is a third of the way around with a working fleet. Then what to do first, in steps that cost conversations rather than licenses. Then what to have ready when agents arrive anyway, who decides what, and the one line that must hold: used for residents, never on them. The close reveals that the plan was the four lessons applied, and that the City's own staff laid most of the ground.

**You say:** The word agent gets used for everything, so here is the definition we will hold to. In 2023 a chatbot answered a question. A copilot drafts while a person decides, one step at a time. ## Part 3. The four lessons (about 5 minutes)

### 7. Lesson one: the least autonomy that gets the job done

Speaker: Stefan

**On screen:** Lesson one. Use the least autonomy that gets the job done.
- Keep the whole range on hand: automation, copilot, workflow, agent. Each step up is more to govern.
- Some jobs need no AI at all. A plain tool, built fast with AI's help, is often the right answer.

**They see:** Four steps rise left to right like a stair, each a block: "Automation" with the caption "Fixed rules", "Copilot" with "A person approves every step", "Workflow" with "The steps are set", and "Agent" with "Chooses its own steps". A small green figure climbs onto the first step and stops; a deep-green tick appears over it with the label "Gets the job done". A grey arrow along the top of the stair reads "More to govern". Below the stair a small tool outline fills solid, labeled "Plain tool", with the caption "No AI at all". The slide ends on the stair, the figure on the first step, the grey arrow and the plain tool.

**You say:** Lesson one is the one we would want every manager in the City to carry around. There is a range of ways to automate work, and it climbs like a stair. Automation runs on fixed rules. A copilot drafts and a person approves every step. In a workflow the steps are set in advance, with a model helping inside them. An agent chooses its own steps. Each step up adds value sometimes and adds things to govern always. So the course says: climb one step at a time, stop as soon as the job gets done, and ask why not less. The point for leadership is that the City needs the whole range on hand, so each job can land on the lowest step that works. And some jobs need no AI at all. A plain tool, built fast with AI's help, is often the right answer.

### 8. Lesson two: build the rules into the system
<!-- beat: lesson two; the enforcement ladder from strongest to weakest, and the dial that fits controls to exposure -->
Speaker: Stefan

**On screen:** Lesson two. Build the rules into the system, where the AI cannot break them.
- If you tell an AI a rule, it can be talked out of it. If the system blocks the action, it cannot.
- Match the controls to the risk: light where AI only assists, heavy where a mistake is hard to undo.

**They see:** Four blocks descend left to right like a stair going down: "The system blocks it", "A person approves first", "The AI is told the rule", and "A person checks afterward". A green arrow at the left reads "Strongest". Below the stair a dial draws with three settings: "AI only assists" with the caption "Light controls", "A person approves" with "Named approvers", and "Costly or hard to undo" with "Heavy controls". The needle rests on the first setting. The slide ends on the descending stair and the dial.

**You say:** Lesson two is where the course was firmest. If you tell an AI a rule, in its instructions, it can be talked out of it; the course cites attacks that got past tested defenses most of the time. If the system blocks the action, with a permission it does not have or a network it cannot reach, the rule holds whether the AI cooperates or not. From strongest to weakest, the controls run: the system blocks it, a person approves first, the AI is told the rule, and a person checks afterward. That last one only counts when the person has the time, the information and the authority to stop what they are looking at. The second half of the lesson is proportion. Where AI only assists, use light controls, approved tools and data and a quick review, so the work keeps moving. In the middle, a person approves, with named approvers. Heavy controls belong where a mistake is costly or hard to undo.

Most first picks score well on all six, and most of them are work where AI only assists. Those are the four lessons. The rest of the talk is where the City stands against the four, and it is a better picture than you might think.
