**How to read it.** Each slide has three parts:

## What the DCIOs should walk away with

1. Four lessons from the course they can repeat in a meeting, and the sense that they understood each one.
2. An accurate picture of where the City stands: where it looks like it is on paper, where it is once its own work is counted and the outside recognition it already has, and where it could be with organization and a budget line. The program walks into a community; it does not start one.
3. A plan whose first steps are already half done, so each decision reads as a short distance rather than a request.
4. A clear sense of their own part: leadership draws the lines AI may never cross, each system's DCIO decides how much it may do on its own, the CISO owns the controls.
5. Confidence in the people who have been doing this work, and in Stefan and Chris as the ones to organize it. The talk never asks for that; the last slide shows that the plan they just heard was the course applied. The one thing only leadership can add is the connection between the pieces, and the talk hands them that as the decision and the credit.

## The story

Two people went to a course and came back with four lessons. Before teaching the lessons, they show what the lessons answer: enterprise has moved to agents, spent heavily, changed how it reviews work, and stumbled wherever a limit was missing. Then the four lessons, one at a time. Then the City: on paper it is at the start line, but once you count the people, the recognition and the infrastructure, it is a third of the way around with a working fleet. Then what to do first, in steps that cost conversations rather than licenses. Then what to have ready when agents arrive anyway, who decides what, and the one line that must hold: used for residents, never on them. The close reveals that the plan was the four lessons applied, and that the City's own staff laid most of the ground.

**You say:** One more thing changed, and it explains why the controls changed with it. When AI was a chatbot, a person read every answer before anything happened, and that was enough. **You say:** One more definition, because it comes back later. A fleet is a team of agents working one job, each with its own role. The course's own demonstration was a fleet of five agents, and it spent as much time on the walls between the agents as on the agents themselves.

## Part 3. The four lessons (about 5 minutes)

### 7. Lesson one: the least autonomy that gets the job done
<!-- beat: lesson one; the ladder, stop at the first rung that works, and many jobs need a plain tool -->
Speaker: Stefan

**On screen:** Lesson one. Use the least autonomy that gets the job done.
- Autonomy is how much the AI does without a person. Four rungs: automation, a copilot, a workflow, an agent.
- Each rung up means more autonomy, and more to control, review and answer for. Stop at the lowest that works.
**They see:** Four steps rise left to right like a stair, each a block: "Automation" with the caption "Fixed rules, no AI", "Copilot" with "A person approves every step", "Workflow" with "People set the steps; AI helps inside them", and "Agent" with "Chooses its own steps". A small green figure climbs onto the first step and stops; a deep-green tick appears over it with the label "Stop here if it gets the job done". A grey arrow along the top of the stair reads "More autonomy, so more to control, review and answer for". Below the stair a small tool outline fills solid, labeled "Plain tool", with the caption "Some jobs need no AI at all". The slide ends on the stair, the figure on the first step, the grey arrow and the plain tool.

**You say:** Lesson one is the one we would want every manager in the City to carry around. There are four rungs, and they climb like a stair. Automation runs on fixed rules, with no AI in it. A copilot drafts and a person approves every step. In a workflow people set the steps in advance, and AI helps inside them. An agent chooses its own steps. Autonomy is how much the AI decides and does without a person. Each rung up gives the AI more of it, and more autonomy means more that someone has to control, review and answer for. So the course says: climb one rung at a time, stop at the lowest rung that gets the job done, and ask why not less. The point for leadership is that the City needs all four rungs available, so each job can land on the lowest one that works. And some jobs need no AI at all. A plain tool, built fast with AI's help, is often the right answer.

### 8. Lesson two: put the rules where the AI cannot break them
<!-- beat: lesson two; the enforcement ladder from strongest to weakest, and the dial that fits controls to exposure -->
Speaker: Stefan

**On screen:** Lesson two. Put the rules where the AI cannot break them.
- If you only tell an AI a rule, a clever request can talk it out of it. If the system blocks the action, it cannot.
- Where AI only assists, approved tools and a quick review are enough. Where a mistake is costly, add approvals.

**They see:** Four blocks descend left to right like a stair going down: "The system blocks it", "A person approves first", "The AI is told the rule", and "A person checks afterward". A green arrow at the left reads "Strongest control". Below the stair a dial draws with three settings: "AI only assists" with the caption "Approved tools and a quick review", "Some risk" with "A named person signs off", and "Costly or hard to undo" with "Approvals and system blocks". The needle rests on the first setting. The slide ends on the descending stair and the dial.

**You say:** Lesson two is where the course was firmest. If you tell an AI a rule, in its instructions, it can be talked out of it; the course cites attacks that got past tested defenses most of the time. If the system blocks the action, with a permission it does not have or a network it cannot reach, the rule holds whether the AI cooperates or not. From the strongest control to the weakest, they run: the system blocks it, a person approves first, the AI is told the rule, and a person checks afterward. That last one only counts when the person has the time, the information and the authority to stop what they are looking at. The second half of the lesson is proportion. Where AI only assists, approved tools and data and a quick review are enough, so the work keeps moving. With some risk, a named person signs off. Where a mistake is costly or hard to undo, you require approvals and you block the risky actions in the system.

Give every agent a named human owner; the course found that many agents have none. At the City's scale that is a list and a set of owners.
